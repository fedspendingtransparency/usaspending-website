You might not always think about it, but federal spending touches nearly every aspect of your life. Imagine all the things you might see if you’re driving down a street in your community: schools, hospitals, public parks, and local businesses. Each of these places can be affected by government spending on topics such as education, public health, business, and environmental protection.

Each year, the U.S. government spends [trillions of dollars](https://www.usaspending.gov/explorer/budget_function) to provide services, fund programs, and invest in communities across the country.

But how does this complex process work? And how does USAspending.gov help you follow the money?

## The Federal Spending Process: A Quick Overview

Federal spending involves a series of steps that includes both the President and Congress and ends with federal agencies distributing funds.

### Step One: Budget Process

The U.S. Constitution gives Congress the ability to create a federal budget—in other words, to determine how much money the government can spend over the course of the upcoming fiscal year.

For a more in-depth look at the budget process, visit the [federal budget process page](https://www.usa.gov/federal-budget-process) on [usa.gov](https://www.usa.gov) and the [federal spending page](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/americas-finance-guide/federal-spending/) on our partner site, [Fiscal Data](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov).

### Step Two: Appropriations

Appropriations is the process by which Congress designates and approves spending for a specific purpose (e.g., a project or program). Most government spending is determined through appropriation bills, which must be passed by Congress and signed into law by the President before agencies can use the allocated funds.

### Step Three: Obligations

Each year, the Treasury Department issues funds to federal agency spending accounts (known as [Treasury Accounts](https://www.usaspending.gov/data-sources?glossary=treasury-account-symbol-tas)) as a result of appropriations from Congress. 

Agencies enter into binding agreements called <GlossaryLink term="obligation" label="obligations" /> to spend the appropriated money for certain authorized purposes. These purposes could be for agency expenses or for federal awards such as contracts, grants, and loans. Obligations could commit the agency to spend money immediately or in the future, but it’s important to note that obligations don’t always result in payments being made.

### Step Four: Outlays

<GlossaryLink term="outlay" label="Outlays" /> occur when federal agencies authorize payments to individuals, businesses, or other organizations. Whereas an obligation is merely a promise to spend money, an outlay represents actual spending. Agencies execute programs, monitor spending, and report back regularly on how funds are being used.

USAspending.gov has granular spending data at the award level including grants, contracts, and loans. Our sister site, [FiscalData.Treasury.gov](http://www.fiscaldata.treasury.gov/), provides a big-picture view of how the government manages its money. Fiscal Data contains datasets like the Daily and Monthly Treasury Statements, which contain detailed information on receipts and outlays.
